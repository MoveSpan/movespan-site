MoveSpan System
🌙 Sleep Reset
18 minutes · metronome guides the pace · follow at your own rhythm

The programme rests on one idea: the mind follows rhythm. A steady external pulse gives attention something simple to hold, and thinking gradually loosens its grip.

The tempo drops in stages — 40 → 30 → 20 BPM. Each drop asks the body to slow down a little more: breathing lengthens, the pulse eases, the nervous system shifts out of alert mode.

On top of the rhythm sit the MoveSpan practices — foot work, acupressure and extended breathing. Together they turn 18 minutes into a reliable way into sleep, without forcing anything.

Optional — skip it if you are tired. If you have the energy, massage your feet while the metronome keeps running. Two ways:
🎾 With a ball (easier): place a massage or tennis ball under the foot. Sit and roll it slowly along the sole — heel to toes and back. 1–2 min per foot.
🖐 With your hands: oil on the palms (coconut, sesame or olive). Thumbs first in circles, then press and hold. Breathe out on each press.

Stay with the beat. Press each point softly — no force. Breathe out as you press.
👈 Between the brows (Yintang)
Middle of the forehead, in the small hollow just above the bridge of the nose. Press with one fingertip, hold 20–30 seconds, release slowly. Repeat 2–3 times. Eyes closed throughout.
👑 Crown of the head (Bai Hui)
Top of the skull, where a line from the tips of both ears would meet. Small circles with two fingers, or simply rest the palm there for 30 seconds.
Both points sit where tension gathers when the mind will not stop. Steady pressure with a long exhale slows the pulse and softens the face — the jaw, the brow, the muscles around the eyes. The mind tends to follow the face.

Extended Breath (Svistyelka) — from the exercise library.
• Lie down — jaw loose, legs relaxed
• Free inhale through the nose
• Free exhale through the mouth with a soft «ssss» — the teeth and tongue slow the air, so the exhale stretches on its own
• Don’t count and don’t pull the breath longer on purpose. The practice is to relax on the exhale — the length comes by itself
• As the body lets go, each exhale gets longer and easier. The sound follows it: louder and rougher at first, then quieter, softer, almost a whisper
• Let the sound tell you where you are. When it goes soft, you are close to sleep
🌙 After the last breath — let go completely. Stop controlling the breath. Let it breathe itself. You may fall asleep here.

🎵 Metronome as a practice

Our body lives in constant rhythms — breathing, heartbeat, steps, sleep cycles. Under stress or emotional overload these rhythms become less organised: thoughts speed up, attention scatters, and the body finds it harder to let go.

A metronome creates a steady, predictable beat that can serve as an external anchor for attention. Instead of chasing an endless stream of thoughts, the mind begins returning, again and again, to a calm repeating sound. Many people notice that after just a few minutes the inner tension eases, breathing settles, and the body begins to relax.

Different tempos serve different purposes:

60 BPM A foundational rhythm close to a resting heartbeat. A natural starting point — good for beginners getting used to practising with a metronome.
50 BPM Well suited for breathwork and for breathing practices embedded in exercises such as Strong Body Yoga.
40 BPM Helps reduce inner restlessness and shift into a calmer state.
30 BPM Supports deeper relaxation and a quiet, gathered attention.
20 BPM Many experience this as an almost meditative rhythm — helpful for preparing for sleep or deep relaxation.

These are reference points, not rules. Trust your own sense of what feels right — 43, 37, 45 BPM are all valid. Experiment and find the tempo that works for you in this moment.

While listening, there is no need to concentrate on each beat. Simply turn it on in the background and let the rhythm gradually become part of the surrounding space. It pairs well with foot massage, head self-massage, calming exercises and evening wind-down rituals.

The metronome does not force the nervous system to relax. It creates a stable external rhythm relative to which the mind finds it easier to organise attention, breathing and inner processes. When the chaos settles, relaxation often arises on its own.

Use the metronome as a simple tool for restoring your inner rhythm — a gentle way to move from constant activity into calm, presence and rest.
