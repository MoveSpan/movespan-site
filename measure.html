At rest, a healthy breathing rate is 12–16 breaths per minute. Above 16–18 means the system is chronically rushing even when there's nothing to rush for.

Fast breathing almost always means chest breathing — the diaphragm isn't fully engaging. This creates a loop: chest breathing activates the sympathetic nervous system → stress → chest breathing. The diaphragm loses mobility, internal abdominal pressure rises, organs get less massage from breathing movements.

Biochemically: when you over-breathe, CO₂ leaves the blood too fast, blood vessels narrow — paradoxically, you feel like you can't get enough air even though oxygen is fine. This is the internal compression that joint gymnastics and breathwork directly address.

One cycle of slow nasal breathing with belly movement switches the nervous system from sympathetic to parasympathetic in 2–3 minutes. Svistyelka, slow exhales, full diaphragmatic breath — these are the tools.

The diaphragm is the primary muscle of breathing. When it moves freely, the lower ribs and abdomen usually participate naturally.

During prolonged stress, breathing often shifts upward toward the chest. The neck, shoulders, and upper chest begin doing more work, while movement around the lower ribs and abdomen becomes smaller.

This check does not diagnose a medical condition. It helps you notice your current movement pattern and choose practices for diaphragm mobility, rib expansion, and relaxation.

Resting pulse shows how hard your heart works when the body is doing nothing. The higher it is, the more effort your system spends just maintaining baseline.

A high resting pulse means the sympathetic nervous system («fight or flight») is dominant — blood vessels are narrowed, muscles slightly tensed, internal pressure elevated. The body is bracing for a threat that isn't there. This is chronic background stress — most people don't notice it because they've come to accept it as normal.

Our practices lower resting pulse by activating the vagus nerve — Svistyelka, slow exhales, joint gymnastics with breath, Shavasana. This is why long-term yoga practitioners often have a resting pulse of 45–55 bpm: not from running, but from teaching the nervous system to truly rest.
