Your Breath Snapshot
Your measurements

Your Breath Snapshot

These measurements describe three different parts of your current resting state. They are most useful when viewed together and repeated under similar conditions.

Breathing rate describes tempo

Your breathing rate shows how many complete breathing cycles occur in one minute. It describes breathing frequency, but does not show where movement occurs or how much effort is used.

Breathing pattern describes movement

Your breathing pattern describes where you noticed the clearest movement: higher in the chest, through the lower chest, around the abdomen and lower ribs, or mainly in the lower abdomen.

Resting pulse describes heart rate

Resting pulse shows how many times the heart beats per minute during this measurement. It can vary with recent activity, heat, hydration, caffeine, sleep, illness, medication, emotional state, and individual fitness.

A single Breath Snapshot is an observation, not a diagnosis. Repeated measurements under similar conditions are more useful for identifying your personal baseline and changes over time.

Why Breathing Matters
Whole-body function

Why Breathing Matters

Breathing is not only the movement of air into and out of the lungs. Each breath also involves the diaphragm, ribs, spine, abdominal wall, shoulders, neck, posture, and the way the body coordinates pressure and movement.

1 The diaphragm creates movement During quiet inhalation, the diaphragm descends while the abdomen and lower ribs can expand in several directions.
2 The rib cage must remain adaptable The ribs need enough mobility to expand forward, sideways, and backward without excessive lifting through the upper chest.
3 The spine and shoulders influence breathing Thoracic stiffness, rounded posture, restricted shoulders, or persistent neck tension may change how freely the ribs move.
4 The abdominal wall works with the diaphragm The abdomen is not simply relaxed or tightened. It adapts during breathing, movement, posture, and pressure regulation.
5 Breathing changes with your state Activity, temperature, posture, discomfort, attention, emotion, sleep, and illness may all influence breathing frequency and movement.

Why relaxation alone may not be enough

A person can understand how to breathe and still have mechanical restrictions through the lower ribs, thoracic spine, shoulders, abdominal wall, or tissues around the rib margins. This is why MoveSpan combines breathing awareness with mobility, release, stretching, and coordinated movement.

Why stronger breathing is not always better

The goal is not to maximise every inhalation. A useful resting breath is generally quiet, comfortable, adaptable, and appropriate for the person’s current activity and condition.

Breathing observations can help guide general wellness practices, but they do not diagnose respiratory, cardiac, neurological, or musculoskeletal conditions.

Your Breathing Pattern
Your movement pattern

Your Breathing Pattern

A breathing pattern describes where you notice the clearest movement during a calm, natural breath. It is an observation of movement, not a diagnosis.

Do not correct the breath too quickly

First observe what the body currently does. Forcing the abdomen outward, deliberately lifting the chest, or repeatedly taking larger breaths can hide the person’s natural resting pattern.

The pattern can change

Breathing movement may vary with position, recent activity, fatigue, pain, attention, emotion, temperature, and mobility. Repeating the observation in similar conditions is more useful than treating one result as permanent.

Meet Your Diaphragm
Your main breathing muscle

Meet Your Diaphragm

The diaphragm is a broad, dome-shaped muscle located beneath the lungs. It forms a moving boundary between the chest and abdominal cavities and plays the central mechanical role in quiet breathing.

What happens when you inhale?

During inhalation, the diaphragm contracts and moves downward. This creates more space inside the chest while pressure changes allow air to enter the lungs. The abdomen may move gently forward, and the lower ribs can expand to the sides and back.

What happens when you exhale?

During a quiet exhalation, the diaphragm relaxes and returns upward toward its dome shape. The rib cage and abdominal wall recoil naturally. Exhalation should not always require strong abdominal contraction.

Why the lower ribs matter

The diaphragm attaches around the lower rib cage. Its movement is therefore connected with the ability of the lower ribs to widen forward, sideways, and backward. A mobile diaphragm is not expressed only through movement of the front abdomen.

Belly movement is only one part

Diaphragmatic breathing does not mean deliberately pushing the abdomen outward. Natural abdominal movement should occur together with comfortable lower-rib expansion and without excessive lifting through the shoulders or upper chest.

What can limit comfortable movement?

Breathing movement may be influenced by stiffness through the lower ribs and thoracic spine, tension around the rib margins, persistent abdominal bracing, posture, recent pain, stress, inactivity, or habitual upper-chest breathing.
